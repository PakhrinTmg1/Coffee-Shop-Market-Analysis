Coffee Shop Market Analysis

Coffee retail is a consumer-driven market full of lessons about choice, uncertainty, costs and competition. This paper applies microeconomic theory to ask one question: should I open a coffee shop?

Choice with certainty

Why people buy coffee, and how they react to price

Coffee delivers taste, caffeine, a social experience and brand identity. Buyers pay for its function (alertness, energy) and its lifestyle meaning (status, routine). Segments run from budget instant-coffee buyers to premium specialty drinkers.

These choices follow rational preferences. They are complete: a drinker can always compare coffee with tea, boba or energy drinks and say which they prefer, or that they are equal. They are also transitive: if someone prefers specialty to instant coffee, and instant coffee to tea, they will prefer specialty coffee to tea.

Maximum willingness to pay for brew coffee is $15, and the average price is around $6–7. Demand is elastic because close substitutes are everywhere.

Complements

  • Sugar

Substitutes

Substitution vs. income effect

A coffee price rise triggers both effects, but not equally. With many close substitutes, consumers switch easily, so the substitution effect is strong. Coffee is a small share of most budgets (perhaps 2–5%), so people do not feel meaningfully poorer and the income effect is small. Through the Slutsky decomposition, a price rise pushes people toward substitutes far more than it makes them cut back from lost purchasing power.

Worked example

Budget is $100, coffee (x) costs $5 and coffee alternatives (y) cost $4. At the optimum the MRS equals the price ratio, so y = 5x/4. The budget line gives 5x + 4(5x/4) = 100, so the original bundle is A = (10, 12.5).

Low bean production this year lifts coffee to $7. Then y = 7x/4 and 7x + 4(7x/4) = 100, so the new bundle is B = (7.14, 12.5).

To afford the original bundle at $7 would take an extra (7 − 5) × 10 = $20. With a $120 budget, 7x + 4y = 120 and y = 7x/4 give the compensated bundle (8.57, 15).

The numbers look similar for coffee itself, but substitution is the stronger force in practice. When coffee gets pricier, people still want caffeine. They switch to cheaper tea or energy drinks rather than quit.

Choice with uncertainty

What buyers and sellers do when they can't be sure

Uncertainty for consumers

  • Quality: will the coffee be fresh, strong and as expected? Unfamiliar brands and new shops raise this risk.
  • Ethical sourcing: shoppers care about sustainability but may not know if the beans are Fair Trade, organic or responsibly grown.
  • Future value: will the coffee deliver the energy boost again tomorrow, or was it a one-time experience?

How consumers reduce it

They spend money and time through brand loyalty (paying more for trusted names), certifications such as organic or Fair Trade, and reviews. The drivers are loss aversion and framing: people fear a bad purchase more than they enjoy a great one, and a shop that frames itself as ethical or eco-friendly wins over an identical competitor. Many buyers feel the extra cost buys peace of mind, consistent quality and a sense of supporting ethical practice. Reassurance from branding, certification or positive framing shapes choices even when the product is the same.

How suppliers reduce it

  • Quality control: training baristas, standardized roasting, freshness guarantees.
  • Predictable pricing: loyalty programs, daily discounts and subscriptions that bring customers back even if preferences shift (time inconsistency).
  • Insurance and contracts: long-term supplier agreements and weather insurance to stabilize supply.

These cost real money in training, logistics, premiums and certification fees. Suppliers accept that because committing to quality today builds loyalty, and because customers overweight rare bad experiences (probability weighting), so one mistake can be very costly.

Effect on supply and demand

Demand side: with an unknown brand, buyers doubt quality and safety, so willingness to pay falls and the demand curve shifts left from D to D₁. Supply side: price volatility, crop failure and climate risk raise marginal costs, so the supply curve shifts up from S to S₁. Suppliers need higher prices to supply the same quantity.

Production and costs

What it takes to run a shop

Fixed costs

  • Rent
  • Espresso machine and brewing equipment

Variable costs

  • Labor: baristas, shift managers, cleaning
  • Utilities: water, electricity
  • Ingredients: beans, milk

Short run

The supply curve is the marginal cost curve above AVC. It is steep because short-run supply is inelastic: a shop has fixed capacity and can serve only so many customers per hour. When prices rise it cannot instantly make more cups, because equipment is fixed and hiring and training baristas takes time.

AVC is U-shaped. It falls at first as production becomes more efficient, then rises under diminishing returns. The bottom of the curve is the most efficient output, so I should aim to produce there. If price falls below AVC, I should shut down because even variable costs aren't covered. ATC sits above AVC because it adds average fixed cost, and where price exceeds ATC at the MC intersection, the shop makes a profit.

In the long run, supply becomes flatter (more elastic) because I can open new stores, buy equipment and train staff.

Capital and labor mix

My shop will be a modern café like Starbucks, so I plan on about 70% capital and 30% labor.

Capital 70%Labor 30%

Why 70% capital: coffee is capital intensive. Machines grind, brew, steam milk and refrigerate supplies faster and more consistently than hand work. Mobile ordering, automated inventory and digital POS let me serve more people without adding labor in proportion.

Why 30% labor: the human touch matters. Baristas give personal service, shape the atmosphere, maintain machines, handle exceptions and keep quality up.

Innovation and policy shocks

Short run (capital fixed)

  • AI adoption: cut labor 5–10% as AI handles repetitive work.
  • Minimum wage rise: trim shifts on slow days and push labor productivity.
  • Tariffs: higher ingredient costs, no immediate change to the mix.
  • Rate hikes: pricier borrowing, same capital/labor mix.

Long run (both adjust)

  • AI and automation: MPK rises, mix moves toward 80/20.
  • Lower rates: easier to invest in new technology, shifting further toward capital.
  • Higher wages or shortages: replace expensive labor with cheaper, more reliable capital.

Models of competition

Market structure: monopolistic competition

Beans are a commodity, but the finished product is not identical. Taste, branding, atmosphere and experience differ, so Starbucks and a local café sell similar drinks with distinct vibes.

Barriers to entry are moderate. Anyone can start a shop and get bean supply information, but good locations are mostly held by big firms, and building loyalty in a crowded market makes scaling hard. Information is mostly perfect because prices are visible online. Unlike perfect competition, shops set their own prices based on costs, brand identity and target audience.

Demand for my shop is elastic because of many competitors, from Starbucks to small cafés. Differentiation in taste, ambience and environment still gives me some pricing power, though little, which is why marginal revenue sits close to demand. The gap between price and MC at the profit-maximizing quantity is my markup.

Conclusion

Entering the market looks viable

High demand, flexible pricing and room to differentiate make coffee a strong market for a new business. Even among many competitors, a shop can stand out through taste, atmosphere and branding and keep prices above average total cost. If costs are managed well and trends are followed, the potential for producer surplus is strong. Based on this analysis, becoming a coffee shop owner looks like a good choice.
